# 🌿 Algae Biofertilizer Research

A research and engineering design project completed during ENGR100 at Bucknell University. Our team investigated how green algae grown in animal wastewater could be repurposed into biofertilizers — offering an environmentally friendly solution to nutrient pollution caused by concentrated animal feeding operations (CAFOs).

> Professor Gwin — ENGR100

## 🔧 Technologies & Methods

- Microscopy (cell counting & algae sampling)
- Lab data collection & analysis
- Flocculation (natural harvesting process)
- Scientific graphing & data visualization

---

## 🌍 The Problem

Animal waste from farms is stored in manure lagoons, which can leak and pollute surrounding ecosystems. These lagoons are packed with excess nitrogen and phosphorus. Green algae is commonly introduced into these lagoons to consume those nutrients — but the algae itself can overgrow and cause algal blooms, making bodies of water hypoxic and harmful to aquatic life.

Our team set out to find a way to repurpose that algae rather than let it become another environmental problem.

---

## ✨ What We Did

- **Evaluated three alternatives** for repurposing algae: biofertilizers, bioplastics, and biofuels — weighing the pros and cons of each
- **Selected biofertilizers** as the most beneficial option, as algae-based fertilizers have been shown to improve fruit yield by 15.7%–29.6% and help remove heavy metals from soil and water
- **Grew algae (Chlorella) in the lab** and tracked its growth over time
- **Sampled the algae** and examined it under a microscope to perform cell counts
- **Measured nitrate and phosphate concentrations** over time to confirm the algae was absorbing the excess nutrients from the wastewater
- **Analyzed the data** and created graphs showing algae growth, nutrient absorption, and absorbance levels
- **Presented our findings** at an engineering fair under tight time constraints

---

## ⚙️ The Process

We began by defining the problem — CAFOs produce nutrient-heavy wastewater that can damage ecosystems — and then brainstormed possible solutions. After researching biofertilizers, bioplastics, and biofuels, we evaluated each against criteria like cost, environmental impact, and energy usage.

We selected biofertilizers as the strongest option and moved into the lab phase. We grew Chlorella algae and regularly sampled it, using a microscope to count cells and track growth over time. We also measured the nitrate and phosphate concentrations in the water to confirm the algae was doing its job.

Our data showed that as algae grew, nitrate and phosphate concentrations dropped — proving the algae was actively absorbing the excess nutrients. We plotted this data into graphs and presented our full findings at an engineering design fair.

## ❓ How Can It Be Improved?

- **Test Multiple Algae Types:** We only worked with one type of algae (Chlorella). Testing additional species would help determine which is most efficient at absorbing nutrients and producing usable fertilizer.
- **Expand the Dataset:** More data points collected over a longer period would make our graphs more accurate and our conclusions more reliable.
- **Narrow the Problem Statement:** Our problem statement was broad. A more focused scope would make the research easier to communicate to a general audience.
- **Research Better Harvesting Methods:** The current harvesting process (flocculation) works but is energy intensive. Exploring more efficient and cost-effective drying and harvesting techniques would make the biofertilizer more practical to produce at scale.
